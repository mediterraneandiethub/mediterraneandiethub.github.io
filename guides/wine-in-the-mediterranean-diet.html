Wine in the Mediterranean Diet

Wine is part of the Mediterranean tradition, drunk in moderation with meals, but it is also one of the most misunderstood parts of the diet. Some people think the diet requires wine; others think it forbids it. Neither is true. This guide explains the traditional role of wine, what the evidence says, and how to approach it sensibly.

The traditional role of wine

In Mediterranean cultures, wine is drunk with food, in small amounts, and usually as part of a shared meal. A glass of red wine with lunch or dinner is the traditional pattern, not drinking on an empty stomach or drinking to get drunk. Wine is part of the social and cultural fabric — it accompanies the meal, it is shared, and it is enjoyed slowly. This context matters: the pattern is moderate, regular, and always with food, which is very different from binge drinking or drinking alone.

What the evidence says

Studies have found that moderate wine consumption, especially red wine with meals, is associated with a lower risk of heart disease. Red wine contains polyphenols such as resveratrol, which may protect blood vessels. However, the evidence is not as strong as the evidence for the diet as a whole, and some of the apparent benefit may come from the overall lifestyle of moderate drinkers rather than the wine itself. Alcohol is also a known risk factor for several cancers and for liver disease, and the risks rise with the amount drunk. The healthiest amount of alcohol is none, and nobody should start drinking for health reasons.

Moderation in practice

If you already drink, moderation means up to one glass a day for women and up to two for men, always with food, and not every day if you can avoid it. A glass is about 150 millilitres, not a large goblet filled to the brim. Choose red wine if you drink, since it has the most polyphenols, but do not treat it as medicine. Keep some alcohol-free days each week, and avoid drinking when you are tired, stressed, or alone. If you find it hard to stop at one glass, the safest choice is not to drink at all.

Alcohol-free alternatives

You do not need wine to follow the Mediterranean diet. The diet works perfectly well without alcohol, and many people in Mediterranean countries do not drink. If you want something special with a meal, try sparkling water with lemon and herbs, a non-alcoholic wine, or a homemade shrub — a vinegar-based drink mixed with sparkling water. A glass of water is the traditional accompaniment to a meal, and it is the healthiest choice. The point of the Mediterranean diet is the food, the company, and the pace of the meal, not the alcohol.

Wine regions of the Mediterranean

The Mediterranean is home to some of the world's great wine regions. Chianti in Tuscany, Rioja in Spain, and the wines of the Douro in Portugal are all famous, and they are made from local grape varieties that have grown there for centuries. Greek wines, made from grapes such as Assyrtiko and Agiorgitiko, are increasingly well known. If you drink wine, choosing a wine from the region whose food you are eating is a pleasant tradition — a Chianti with pasta, a Rioja with tapas, a Greek white with grilled fish. Visit a wine region if you can, and taste the wines where they are made; it is a memorable way to learn about the food and culture of the Mediterranean.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to drink wine on the Mediterranean diet?

No. Wine is optional, and the diet works perfectly well without it. Nobody should start drinking for health reasons.

How much wine is moderate?

Up to one glass a day for women and two for men, always with food. A glass is about 150 millilitres. Keep some alcohol-free days each week.

Is red wine good for the heart?

Moderate red wine with meals is associated with better heart health in some studies, but the evidence is not conclusive, and alcohol also carries risks. The overall diet matters far more than the wine.
