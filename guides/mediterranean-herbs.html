Mediterranean Herbs

Herbs are the soul of Mediterranean cooking. They provide flavour without salt or calories, and they are rich in antioxidants and aromatic compounds. A handful of basil, a sprig of rosemary, or a pinch of oregano can transform a simple dish. This guide covers the essential Mediterranean herbs, how to use them, and how to grow them at home.

Basil, oregano, and parsley

Basil is the herb of summer: sweet, peppery, and essential for pesto, tomato salads, and pasta. It is best added at the end of cooking, since heat dulls its flavour. Oregano is the herb of Greece and Italy, with a warm, slightly bitter taste that suits tomato sauces, grilled meat, and salads. Dried oregano is often stronger than fresh, so use it sparingly. Parsley is the workhorse herb: flat-leaf parsley adds freshness to salads, soups, and fish, and it is a key ingredient in tabbouleh. Keep a bunch of parsley in the fridge at all times.

Rosemary, thyme, and sage

Rosemary is a woody herb with a pine-like flavour that suits roast potatoes, lamb, chicken, and bread. Use it sparingly, since it is strong, and remove the woody stems before serving. Thyme is gentler and more versatile: it works in soups, stews, roasted vegetables, and fish, and it holds up well to long cooking. Sage has a warm, earthy flavour and is classic with beans, squash, and pork. These three herbs are perennials in warm climates, and they dry well, so you can keep them in the cupboard all year.

Mint, dill, and bay

Mint is used in both sweet and savoury Mediterranean dishes: in salads, with yoghurt, in teas, and in desserts. It is refreshing and bright, and it grows vigorously, so keep it in a pot. Dill is essential in Greek cooking, especially with fish, yoghurt, and cucumber. Bay leaves add depth to soups, stews, and beans, and they should be removed before serving. A single bay leaf, simmered in a pot of lentils or a tomato sauce, adds a subtle richness that is hard to identify but easy to miss.

Growing and storing herbs

Most Mediterranean herbs grow well in pots on a sunny windowsill or balcony. Basil needs warmth and regular watering, while rosemary, thyme, and sage prefer dry soil and full sun. Harvest herbs in the morning, when their oils are strongest, and use them fresh when you can. To store fresh herbs, wrap them in a damp cloth and keep them in the fridge. To dry them, hang small bunches upside down in a dark, airy place, then store the leaves in jars. Dried herbs keep for six months to a year, but they lose flavour over time, so replace them regularly.

Herbs in drinks and desserts

Herbs are not only for savoury cooking. Mint is classic in tea, and it is refreshing in cold water with lemon. Basil works in a strawberry salad or a lemon dessert. Rosemary can flavour olive oil cakes and shortbread. Lavender, used sparingly, adds a floral note to biscuits and ice cream. Sage can be fried crisp and used as a garnish for squash dishes. Bay leaves flavour custards and rice puddings. Experimenting with herbs in sweet dishes and drinks is a good way to use a glut of herbs from the garden, and it adds a surprising depth of flavour to simple recipes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which herbs are essential for Mediterranean cooking?

Basil, oregano, parsley, rosemary, and thyme cover most dishes. Add mint and dill for Greek food, and bay leaves for soups and stews.

Should I use fresh or dried herbs?

Use fresh herbs for salads and finishing, and dried herbs for long-cooked dishes like soups and stews. Dried oregano and thyme are often stronger than fresh.

How do I store fresh herbs?

Wrap them in a damp cloth or paper towel and keep them in the fridge. Basil is an exception — it turns black in the fridge, so keep it at room temperature in a glass of water.
