Olive Oil Guide

Olive oil is the heart of Mediterranean cooking. It is the main fat in the diet, the base of dressings and sauces, and a source of the monounsaturated fats and polyphenols linked with the diet's health benefits. Yet supermarket shelves are confusing, with grades, origins, and prices that are hard to compare. This guide explains what matters and how to use olive oil well.

Grades of olive oil

Extra virgin olive oil is the highest grade. It is made by cold-pressing olives without heat or chemicals, and it must have no defects in flavour. It has the most polyphenols and the strongest taste. Virgin olive oil is similar but allows small defects. Refined olive oil, often labelled simply olive oil or light olive oil, is made by refining lower-quality oil; it has a neutral taste and fewer polyphenols. For dressings and finishing, extra virgin is always the best choice. For high-heat cooking, a refined or mild extra virgin oil works well.

Choosing a good bottle

Look for extra virgin olive oil in a dark glass bottle or a tin, since light damages the oil. Check the harvest date if it is printed, and choose the freshest bottle you can find — olive oil is best within eighteen months of harvest. A peppery, slightly bitter taste is a sign of fresh, polyphenol-rich oil, not a fault. Price is a rough guide: very cheap extra virgin oil is unlikely to be genuine. Buy a smaller bottle if you use oil slowly, so it stays fresh.

Cooking with olive oil

Olive oil is more heat-stable than its reputation suggests. Extra virgin olive oil has a smoke point around 190 to 210 degrees Celsius, which is fine for sautéing, roasting, and shallow frying. It is not ideal for very high-heat deep frying, where a refined oil is a better choice. Use it generously in dressings, drizzle it over cooked vegetables and soups, and brush it on bread instead of butter. Remember that it is calorie-dense — about 120 calories per tablespoon — so measure it when you are watching your weight.

Storing olive oil

Store olive oil in a cool, dark cupboard, away from the stove and out of direct sunlight. Keep the cap tightly closed, because oxygen speeds up spoilage. Never store it in the fridge, where it can turn cloudy and thick. Once opened, use a bottle within six to eight weeks for the best flavour. If oil smells stale, waxy, or like crayons, it has gone rancid and should be thrown away. Buying smaller bottles more often is better than keeping one large bottle for a year.

Tasting olive oil

Tasting olive oil is a good way to learn what you like. Pour a small amount into a glass, warm it in your hands, and smell it — good oil smells grassy, fruity, or peppery. Sip it and let it coat your mouth; you should taste fruit, a little bitterness, and a peppery catch at the back of your throat. The peppery sensation is a sign of polyphenols, and it is a good thing. Try two or three oils side by side to compare them, and note which you prefer for salads and which for cooking. A tasting is also a pleasant way to spend an afternoon with friends, and it will change how you choose olive oil for ever.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it safe to cook with extra virgin olive oil?

Yes. Its smoke point of around 190 to 210 degrees Celsius is high enough for sautéing, roasting, and shallow frying. Only very high-heat deep frying calls for a refined oil.

What does cold-pressed mean?

It means the oil was extracted without heat, which preserves flavour and polyphenols. Extra virgin olive oil must be cold-pressed by definition.

How can I tell if olive oil is good quality?

Fresh extra virgin oil smells grassy or fruity and tastes slightly bitter and peppery. A dark bottle, a recent harvest date, and a fair price are also good signs.
